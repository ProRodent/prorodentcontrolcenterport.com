The reason for the sequence.

The sequence is trapping, sealing and then cleanup. Doing it in the wrong order wastes money, because animals that remain will foul fresh insulation. We confirm quiet before we start removing anything.

A crawl space or attic can hold a lot of material. Insulation gets shredded into nests, soaked with urine and dotted with droppings along every joist. The result smells, loses its insulating value and can feed a lingering odor.

What comes out and what is kept.

  • Insulation that is matted, stained or mixed with droppings.
  • Nest piles of paper, fabric and fibers, bagged on the spot.
  • Droppings along joists, tops of walls and ductwork.
  • Hard-to-clean porous items, which we flag for you to decide on.

Clean, dry batting that sits undisturbed often stays. We explain what we see before pulling anything, since replacement material is an added cost you may not need.

How we keep dust down.

Dry sweeping and vacuuming send fine particles into the air, which is exactly what the CDC steps warn against. The space gets fresh air first, and the droppings are soaked in disinfectant before anything is lifted. Material is bagged where it lies and carried out in sealed bags.

When the debris is out, surfaces are treated and the cavity is ventilated. We wear protective gear and recommend you stay out of the area while work runs.

Before you book cleanup.

Confirm that trapping has ended and that exclusion work is finished. If chewed ducting, wiring covers or drywall are present, ask about rodent damage repair.

Questions about your health after exposure belong with your doctor. The CDC pages linked below describe safe cleanup in more detail.

Temporary precautions.

  • A vehicle with debris beneath the bonnet should stay parked until it has been inspected.
  • Birdseed, grass seed and kibble come indoors for now.
  • Drop anything gnawed into a bag so the technician can examine it.
  • Locate the electrical panel in case a cable turns out to be bitten through.

Household health checklist while you wait

No phone call is needed to start. Health departments hand out this same guidance to anybody who spots droppings indoors.

  • A fast dry sweep looks harmless but lofts particles that hover for ages.
  • Trapped air holds whatever has been floating in it, so let a long-sealed space clear out first.
  • Keep gloved fingers away from your face, and wash once the gloves are off.
  • The routine runs spray, rest, wipe, and the rest lasts about five minutes.
  • Tie the bag, put it in the bin, then give your hands another wash.
  • Over the next few weeks, keep an eye on how you feel and tell a doctor about flu-like aches or labored breathing.

Questions about cleanup.

Why is cleanup not the first step?

While animals still use the space, new insulation would be soiled once more. We trap and seal first, then clean.

Do you remove all the insulation?

Only what is soiled, matted or contaminated. Clean batting can often stay, though we explain the reasoning on site.

Is the dust dangerous?

Droppings and nesting material can carry germs, which is why the CDC recommends wet methods and protective gear. We follow that approach and suggest you avoid the space meanwhile.

Will cleanup remove the odor?

It removes the material that holds odor. If a carcass is hidden elsewhere, the smell may persist until it is found.

Can I do the cleanup myself?

Small jobs in open spaces can be done with care, using the CDC steps linked on this page. Attics and crawl spaces are tight and dusty, which makes them harder than they look.

How an odor arises.

A rodent that dies inside a wall or ceiling decomposes out of sight. The smell is strongest in the first week or two, and it can drift through vents and cracks. Poison often plays a role, because a poisoned animal seeks shelter and dies there.

The odor can seem to move. Heating and cooling air carry it through ducts, and a closed door can trap it. That is why we ask when the smell is strongest and which rooms it reaches.

How we track it down.

We follow the smell with our noses and a moisture meter. Dead animals are often near warm pipes, behind insulation or in the space between floors. Flies and stained drywall are clues.

  • Walk the strongest room and test along the walls and ceiling.
  • Check attic, crawl space and any access panel nearby.
  • Look for stains, flies or droppings that point to the area.
  • Open only the smallest access needed to reach the source.

Taking it out and treating the spot.

When we find the carcass, we use gloves or tools to lift it and drop it into a bag. The area is treated with a cleaner that breaks down odor rather than a perfume that hides it. Absorbent material that holds the smell may need replacement.

Follow the CDC advice linked below on handling dead rodents. Use gloves, keep bare skin away from it and wash up after any contact.

Poison and odor.

Poison is a common cause of hidden carcasses. A poisoned rodent moves slowly and looks for a quiet place. Dealing with the odor afterward may prove tougher than the first problem was. This is the reason we prefer traps and sealing, in line with the EPA page linked below.

Let the technician know if bait has been laid already. We use the information to search likely hiding places, such as warm spots near the kitchen or utility lines.

A photo of the pellets or chewed spot, described over the phone, helps the visit start sooner.

Preventing a second round.

Once the odor is gone, exclusion work closes the gaps that let animals in. If the carcass fouled insulation, attic and crawl space cleanup may follow. Damaged materials can be handled through rodent damage repair.

In the meantime, ventilate the room, run a fan and avoid scented sprays that only mask the odor. The smell fades once the source is gone.

Holding pattern until we arrive.

  • A vehicle with debris beneath the bonnet should stay parked until it has been inspected.
  • Birdseed, grass seed and kibble come indoors for now.
  • Drop anything gnawed into a bag so the technician can examine it.
  • Locate the electrical panel in case a cable turns out to be bitten through.

Dealing with droppings without a broom

Pausing before you handle anything protects your lungs at almost no cost.

  • Even a filtered vacuum can leak the smallest specks back out, so put it away.
  • Wedge the door, let air flow through and come back in about half an hour.
  • Leave gloves on for the whole job, remove them carefully, rinse reusable ones and wash your hands.
  • Disinfectant works slowly on waste, so be generous with it and take your time wiping.
  • Pick up a dead creature with something between you and it, then place it straight into a bag.
  • Health questions fall outside our expertise. If you feel unwell following exposure, a clinician is the right person.

Odor questions, answered here.

How many days will the smell linger?

A carcass can smell for a few weeks, depending on size and conditions. Removing it ends the source of the odor.

Can I just mask the smell with spray?

Sprays and candles cover the odor for a while. The source remains, and the smell returns.

Will a wall have to be opened?

Only if needed, and only with your permission. We try to reach the source through existing access first, and we explain options for repair.

What brings the flies?

Flies are drawn to carcasses. A sudden group of flies indoors is a clue we ask about.

Can the smell hurt me?

The odor is unpleasant, and decomposing material can carry germs. Wear gloves and avoid contact, and consult a doctor if you feel unwell.

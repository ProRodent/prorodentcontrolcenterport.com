The yard as a highway.

Rodents rarely travel through open lawn. Their routes hug edges: the foot of a fence, a stone wall's length, the dark below a deck or the mulch bed along a foundation. Those edges become a highway, and it leads to your house.

This is why yard work belongs in a rodent plan. A change that cuts food, cover or access outdoors reduces how often rodents test your walls.

What we search for.

  • Fresh soil mounds and burrow openings, often near foundations, walls and sheds.
  • Runways in grass or leaf litter, marked by flattened stems.
  • Food sources such as seed, fruit, pet bowls and compost.
  • Woodpiles, stored lumber and debris stacked against structures.
  • Gnaw marks on decks, shed corners and fence posts.

Ranking the changes.

Not every change matters equally. We rank them by effect and effort, so you can start with the easy wins. Food comes first, then cover, then structure.

Change.Effort.Effect.
Relocate bird feeders well away from the house and sweep up under them.Low.High, because it removes a steady food source.
Raise firewood on a rack, a few feet from the wall.Medium.High, because it removes cover beside the foundation.
Trim shrubs and ivy back from the siding.Medium.Medium, because it opens sightlines and reduces shelter.
Move to a closed compost bin.Low.Medium, because it limits scraps rodents can reach.
Seal gaps under decks and sheds.Higher.High, because it removes ready-made nests.

Think of this as a menu, not a mandate. Your property may need only two or three items. We will say which ones matter most for your layout.

Traps and the long view.

Outdoor traps sit in enclosed boxes along runs and near burrow mouths. We log each location and recheck on named dates. Burrows are left alone until we have seen them, because filling a hole too soon can push the animal to dig closer to the house.

Combine yard work with trapping and exclusion so animals have fewer reasons and fewer routes. If a rat is already using the house, read about rat control for the full plan.

From the call until the knock.

  • Keep a path open to the rear of the lot and warn us of any dog that wanders.
  • Shrubs and vines against the wall should stay as they are until our inspection.
  • A small torch is useful in dim corners.
  • Photograph burrows, pellets and nest remnants before wind or rain rearranges them.

Health agency advice, boiled down

A measured pace keeps fine particles from reaching your lungs.

  • A fast dry sweep looks harmless but lofts particles that hover for ages.
  • Leave a shut-up room thirty minutes with windows wide, and do not hover at the doorway.
  • A pair of gloves keeps your skin clear of droppings, urine and bedding.
  • Wet each pellet and stain thoroughly, then allow the liquid to do its work.
  • Tie the bag, put it in the bin, then give your hands another wash.
  • Should illness set in during the next few weeks, tell the doctor you were around rodents, because our work is limited to pests.

Yard questions from callers.

Do bird feeders cause rat problems?

They can, because fallen seed feeds more than birds. Moving the feeder away from the house and cleaning beneath it helps.

Should I remove my woodpile?

Not necessarily. Raise it on a rack and keep it a few feet from the house, so it does not shelter rodents beside your wall.

Is compost a rodent magnet?

It can be if it contains cooked food or meat. A closed bin with a solid bottom and a lid lowers the risk.

What about dense shrubs and ivy?

They provide cover. Keeping plants trimmed back from the foundation so light reaches the ground makes the area less attractive.

Do burrows always mean rats?

No. Chipmunks, voles and other animals dig too. A technician checks the size, shape and signs before assuming.

Centerport is an unincorporated hamlet within the Town of Huntington in Suffolk County, on Long Island's North Shore. Many lots hold mature trees, and some buildings are decades old. Seams in aging masonry and weathered trim give a mouse more to work with, which makes a slow, careful look at your own house the right place to begin.

Asking whether rodents exist in the area gets you nowhere, since on Long Island they certainly do. What matters is how readily your particular building admits them. A walk around the footing at twilight, torch in hand, tells you more than guessing from the couch.

Which animal are we dealing with?

Picture a pencil, because that is about the opening a mouse can squeeze through. Rats need closer to half an inch. Norway rats keep to ground level and burrow beside footings, slabs and dry-laid stone, whereas roof rats, if present, move along wires and limbs.

Species determines which trap is used, how heavy the metal must be and how the cleanup proceeds, so we identify the animal before putting a price on paper. What you describe by phone is the starting guess, and the on-site inspection either confirms it or corrects it.

What the weather does to their movements.

Falling temperatures are the textbook trigger, but thaws and heavy rain relocate animals as well. Waterlogged soil drives burrowers to drier, higher ground, and a warm house is frequently the driest spot nearby. After the first hard freeze goes by, tour the whole lot for fresh holes, loosened vent covers and leaf drifts heaped against the siding.

  • Test every vent cover and pipe collar for wobble before cold weather, and look at both bottom corners of the garage door.
  • Decant seed, kibble and lawn seed into lidded tubs.
  • Keep stacked wood on a rack away from the house wall.
  • Rake leaves and cut brush back about an arm's length from the foundation.

Until a technician reaches you.

Three modest actions protect your family and preserve the clues. Do not sweep up pellets. Move kids and pets to another part of the house. A trap set earlier must stay precisely in place.

  • Take pictures of pellets and bitten spots with a coin alongside for size.
  • Record the time and the room for every noise.
  • Keep the door shut on whichever room sees the most activity.
  • Avoid scattered poison and sticky boards, which each bring their own complications.

How a typical visit unfolds.

Whether it is one mouse in a cupboard or a colony inside a cellar wall, the job passes through four steps in a fixed order.

Look.

We start at the spot with the heaviest signs, be it attic, cellar, garage or yard, and then walk the outside for pellets and the openings the animals genuinely use.

Catch.

Traps sit along the routes the signs expose, are matched to the species involved and are positioned where kids and pets cannot reach.

Close.

Every hole, vent and gnawed edge is stopped with flashing, steel mesh or a sealant that sets hard against teeth.

Confirm.

We return to empty the traps, pull on each repair to test it and book any remaining cleanup or restoration.

What rodent activity looks like.

When several of these cluster in a single corner, expect live animals and not the remains of an old infestation.

  • Droppings.

    Small dark pellets the size of a grain, found along baseboards, under sinks, across cellar shelves or on top of attic insulation.

  • Fresh gnawing.

    New bite marks look pale and ragged on rafters, pipes, cable jackets and cardboard.

  • Night noises.

    Scratching, pattering or a dull thump in a wall or ceiling, heard when the house settles down.

  • Nest material.

    Shredded paper, pulled-out insulation, fabric or dry leaves piled in a protected corner.

  • Grease trails.

    Greasy dark streaks on walls, pipes and joists reveal a route followed every night.

  • An odd odor.

    Sharp ammonia in a closed room, or a thick sweetish stink that suggests an animal died nearby.

If several of these turn up together, call now, before more is chewed.

Opening questions from callers.

Does anyone answer overnight?

Yes, around the clock including holidays. If today has space, you are offered a same-day visit; if not, you get the earliest opening after that.

How can I distinguish mice from rats?

Droppings the size of rice suggest mice, and larger blunt-ended ones suggest rats. Heavy thumping above you hints at a larger animal. The technician makes the call at your property.

Is it wise to set out poison while waiting?

Generally not at first. Poisoned animals often die within a wall, and removing the odor can be a bigger task than the original problem. The EPA page linked near the bottom outlines the rules.
